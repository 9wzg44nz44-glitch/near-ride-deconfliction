SimulatorMethod & about

Method, data and limits

Estimates only. This tool is a planning aid, not a safety system. It cannot know about weather, mud, washouts, traffic, mechanical trouble, wrong turns, rider choices or road closures. Obey posted speed limits and stop signs on public roads. Class VI roads in New Hampshire are unmaintained by the town; they are used at your own risk, may be closed seasonally or by the town, and their legal status and condition must be verified before riding. Nothing here replaces the judgment of the ride leader and each rider.

How to use

  1. Set riders and departure times. The Scenario table sits above the transport bar and map. Any change (riders, departure, skill, direction, pace, extra stops, terrain edits, stop times, model settings such as the encounter threshold) recalculates the simulation automatically, returns the clock to the earliest departure and shows a status line such as "Simulation updated: 4 encounters found".
  2. Press Run. The transport bar above the map stays on screen while you scroll. It has Run/Pause, Restart (back to the earliest departure), a speed selector (1x, 10x, 60x, 300x; default 300x), the clock in Eastern time and a time slider with encounter marks. "Next encounter" jumps to one minute before the next one.
  3. Watch for pauses at encounters. With "Pause automatically at each encounter" on (the default), playback stops when an encounter begins, the banner names the groups, whether they travel in the same or opposite direction, the terrain and the time, and a pulsing ring marks the spot on the map. Press Continue (or Run) to carry on; the same encounter does not pause again. "Minimum severity" limits the pauses to medium or high severity encounters.

The map legend can be collapsed with its Legend button and starts collapsed on narrow screens. The yellow "How to use" strip on the main page can be dismissed; the "How to use" button under the map brings it back.

What it does

You choose how many riders are on each route and when each group leaves the start. The simulator moves every group along its route second by second, finds the times and places where groups come within a set distance of each other, and labels each encounter by direction (same direction, opposite direction, or crossing), by who is overtaking, and by terrain (paved, maintained dirt, or Class VI). It can then search for start times that avoid or reduce encounters and can run many randomized rides to tell a leader how likely each encounter is.

The routes in this version are SBH Monadnock and MBH Monadnock, two New Hampshire dual-sport routes that share a start and many roads. You can add more routes from a GPX file in the browser (see below).

Data sources

  • Route tracks and waypoints: GPX files from the riders (exported from MapOut). Tracks are simplified to a 2.5 m tolerance for the web page. The waypoints (gas, food, hazards, gates and notes) are parsed and shown on the map and in the Stops tab.
  • Terrain, speed limits and stop signs: OpenStreetMap data downloaded through the Overpass API by the build scripts and stored in the repository. See the attribution below.
  • Map tiles: OpenStreetMap standard tiles, loaded by your browser while you use the page.

Map data © OpenStreetMap contributors, available under the Open Database License (ODbL) 1.0. The derived terrain classification in docs/data/routes.json is a database derived from OpenStreetMap and is shared under the same license. The code is MIT licensed.

How terrain is classified

Each route is sampled every 10 m and each sample is matched to the nearest OpenStreetMap way within 30 m, preferring ways that run in the same direction as the track. Each way gets a class from its tags:

ClassRules used (first match wins)
Class VIName, designation or description mentions Class VI, unmaintained, jeep trail, legal trail, cart path or woods road; or maintenance=no; or highway=path, bridleway or track (unless tagged paved or tracktype grade 1 or 2); or an unpaved road with rough tags (tracktype grade 3 to 5, bad smoothness, ground or mud surface); or an unnamed unpaved road with no speed limit.
Maintained dirtUnpaved surface on a residential, unclassified, service or tertiary road that has a maxspeed tag or tracktype grade 1 or 2, or is a named road; or a track with grade 1 or 2.
PavedPaved surface tags, or a primary, secondary, tertiary or residential road with no surface tag. Unclassified and service roads with no surface tag are assumed paved at low confidence.

Waypoints named like "Jeep Trail", "Class VI", "Cellar hole" or "gate" push nearby unpaved maintained dirt toward Class VI. Gaps with no matching way inherit the neighbor's class. Stretches shorter than 80 m are merged into their neighbors. Every stretch carries a confidence: high (explicit tags), medium (inferred from the road type) or low (weak evidence or gap filling). Posted speed limits come from maxspeed where it exists; otherwise the defaults are 35 mph paved and 25 mph maintained dirt. Class VI has no posted limit.

OpenStreetMap is not perfect. Class VI status is a legal designation that is often missing from OSM, and a road tagged track may be well maintained. Use the Terrain editor on the main page to correct any stretch; edits are saved in your browser and can be exported and imported as JSON.

Speed model

TerrainTarget speed
Class VIIntermediate riders 16 mph average, fast riders 23.5 mph average (22 to 25 mph). The averages already include rough, rocky, hilly and muddy sections, so no extra slowdown is applied on top.
Paved and maintained dirtPosted limit plus 7.5 mph (adjustable 5 to 10; fast riders add 2.5 mph more). Speed is also capped in corners by a lateral acceleration limit (3.0 m/s² intermediate, 3.6 m/s² fast, never below 10 mph), using the curvature of the track.
Acceleration and braking3.0 m/s² accelerating on roads, 2.0 on Class VI, 3.5 braking. Speed ramps are computed forward and backward along the route so riders slow before a stop or a slower stretch.
Stop signs and signalsGroups stop at mapped stop signs (and at inferred junctions where a minor road meets a bigger road, which can be switched off), pause about 2 seconds, and accelerate away. Signals add an expected 20 seconds. Only the direction of travel facing the sign stops.
Regroup stopsGas and food waypoints that lie within 300 m of the track become stops. By default the first stop with food is lunch (45 minutes) and every other gas or food stop is 15 minutes. The whole group waits for the sweep rider, then leaves together. Edit or skip any stop in the Stops tab. Waypoints near the start and end are ignored.

Group dynamics

Each group is a staggered column. The leader is integrated along the route in 10 m cells using the speed profile above. Followers keep a time gap behind the rider ahead: 2 seconds on paved and maintained dirt, 4.5 seconds on Class VI. The gap widens quickly (over about 150 m) on rough ground, closes up more slowly (about 600 m), and grows a little at every stop sign because each rider stops and restarts in turn. A column of 18 riders is therefore several hundred metres long on pavement and longer on Class VI. At a regroup stop the leader waits for the sweep, and the column leaves again with 2 second gaps. Rider positions are then sampled every second for animation and encounter detection. Groups are modeled as a single column; splits into sub-groups are not.

Encounter detection

Every second the simulator knows where the head and the tail of each group are. Two groups are in an encounter when any stretch occupied by one group is within the threshold distance (default 30 m, adjustable 15 to 100 m) of any stretch occupied by the other, measured on the map between route points. This catches shared roads, crossings and merges even where the two routes have different mile counts. Consecutive detections less than 90 seconds apart are merged into one event.

  • Direction: compared by the heading of each route at the contact point: within 60 degrees is same direction, more than 120 degrees is opposite, otherwise crossing or merging.
  • Overtaking: for same-direction events, the order in which the two heads pass the contact point is compared at the start and end of the event. If the order flips, the group that ends up ahead overtook. If not, one group is catching up to or following the other.
  • Terrain: the most common terrain class among the contact points.
  • Riders involved: riders of each group inside the contact zone at the middle of the event.
  • Severity: terrain weight (paved 1, maintained dirt 2, Class VI 4) times relation weight (overtake 2, head-on 2, crossing 1, following 1) times (1 + 0.1 times the riders in both groups). Under 6 is low, 6 to 14 medium, above 14 high. These weights are judgment calls, not measured risk.
  • A group sitting at a gas or food stop counts as being there, so a later group arriving at the same stop shows up as an encounter marked "at a gas/food stop".

Optimizer and leader forecast

Optimizer. Because each group's ride does not depend on when the other group leaves, the simulator rides each group once and then slides it in time. For every pair it scores the weighted severity at every start-time difference in 5 minute steps, then searches all combinations of free groups from 7:00 AM to 3:00 PM (exhaustive for up to three free groups, hill climbing with restarts beyond that). A start-time difference only counts as clear if every difference within 15 minutes either side is also clear. The top options are the most compact schedule, the best schedule with the other departure order, and a further distinct schedule at least 30 minutes away.

Leader forecast. The scenario is simulated many times (30 to 100). Each run multiplies each group's speed by a random factor (about 6 percent) plus a slowly varying local factor (about 7 percent), varies stop and regroup lengths (about 25 percent) and jitters departures by about 2 minutes. Encounters from all runs are grouped by pair and place; the chance is the share of runs containing that encounter, and the time window is the 10th to 90th percentile of its start time. These ranges describe the assumed variability, not a measured one.

Asking questions

The highlighted question card lets you ask the model things like "Are there any head-on encounters?" or "Any encounters on Class VI between 10 and 11 AM?". Choose the event type (any, head-on / opposite direction, same direction, overtake only, crossing or merge), the terrain, group A and group B, the time window (default 7:00 AM to 3:00 PM) and a minimum severity. An event matches if it overlaps the window. For "overtake only", group A is the group that passes and group B the group that is passed; for the other types the two groups are interchangeable and "any group" matches every group. The answer names how many matching events the model has, lists them (click one to jump the map and clock to it) and, after you press "Estimate probability", shows the share of Monte Carlo runs (random speed, stop and start variation) that had at least one matching event. The selection is stored in the share link.

Reality check / calibrate

The "Reality check" tab lets you enter something you actually saw: which route you were on, the mile marker, the approximate time and which group you met. The tool then shows where that mile is (place, terrain, road name), which mile of the other route it corresponds to, whether the other group travels the same or the opposite way there, and when the model has that group's head and tail passing. The difference to your time is turned into an implied shift of the group's departure, an implied pace change, or an implied extra stop. Each of these can be applied to the scenario with one click (as a departure shift, a pace percentage or an extra regroup stop), and the sighting is drawn on the map as a cyan marker with a dashed line to where the model says that group was. The list of flags names the assumptions that disagree: for example, the groups are on opposite sides of a stretch, the model has the group already stopped or not yet arrived, the pace needed is outside what Class VI usually allows, or no stop is modeled before the point.

Related controls: a "Pace %" column per group (a multiplier on all speeds) and per-group "extra stops" (a miles-and-minutes list of regroup stops). Mile markers are drawn on both routes (labels every 1, 5 or 10 miles depending on zoom) and hovering a route shows the mile, terrain and road of every shown route near the pointer. Clicking a route in the Reality check tab fills in the route and mile.

Worked example: the MBH mile 22 sighting

MBH mile 22 is on a Class VI stretch (Annett Road, 42.78084, -71.95921) and is SBH mile 29.1. The SBH route passes that point in the same direction as the MBH route. With the default start times (SBH 18 riders at 9:00, MBH 7 riders at 9:30) the model has the SBH group passing at 9:57 to 9:59 AM and the MBH group at 10:12 AM, a gap of about 14 minutes, so no meeting. Before about MBH mile 40 the two routes share their first 4.4 miles (same direction), a 1.1 km head-on stretch at MBH mile 8.6 to 9.3 (SBH mile 14.1 to 14.8, maintained dirt) and a long same-direction shared road from MBH mile 10.7 (SBH mile 17.8) onward. SBH mile is about MBH mile plus 7. Three separate meetings before 10:35 AM therefore need the groups to be much closer in time than the default assumes, for example SBH regrouping several times on the shared road, a slower SBH pace on Class VI, or an earlier MBH departure. Adding your own routes

In the browser: open "Add GPX" on the main page, choose a file, pick a default terrain and add it. The file is parsed locally and never uploaded. Without OpenStreetMap data the whole route gets one terrain and no stop signs, so use the Terrain editor to mark the Class VI and paved stretches. Imported routes are kept in your browser (when small enough).

In the repository: put GPX files in raw/, list them in scripts/build-routes.mjs, then run npm run build:routes, npm run fetch:osm and npm run build:terrain. This downloads OSM data around the new routes and classifies them automatically. See the README.

Known limitations

  • Speeds are the riders' own typical figures turned into a simple model. Real groups stop for photos, wrong turns, mud, breakdowns and weather, and ride different parts of Class VI at very different speeds.
  • Only about 38 percent of the route length is classified from explicit tags (high confidence); the rest is inferred from road type or filled in. Class VI sections tagged only as track are assumed Class VI at medium confidence.
  • Many rural stop signs are missing from OSM. The inferred junction stops fill part of that gap and are a guess.
  • Time steps are one second. A very fast head-on pass with a threshold under about 20 m between two solo riders could in principle fall between samples.
  • The GPX timestamps are from earlier rides and are not used. Roads change; check closures and seasonal rules.
  • The model has not been calibrated against recorded ride data. Treat the answers as a way to compare options, not as predictions.
